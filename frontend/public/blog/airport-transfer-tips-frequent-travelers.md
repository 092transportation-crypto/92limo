# 10 Airport Transfer Tips Frequent Travelers Swear By

People who fly often develop a set of small habits around ground transportation that make every trip easier — most of it never gets written down because it seems obvious once you know it, and mysterious the first time you don't. Here are ten of those habits, grouped by where they matter most in the trip, from the moment you book through the moment you actually need to reach your chauffeur.

## Before You Fly

1. Book with your actual flight number, not just a time. Flight-number booking is what lets your pickup adjust automatically for delays — a time-only booking can't do that, and it puts the burden of tracking a delay back on you to notice and call about. This one habit alone prevents most of the day-of problems that make airport transfers stressful, because it means the system is already watching your flight before you've even boarded.

2. Confirm your return trip when you book the outbound one. It takes thirty extra seconds and means one less thing to arrange while you're traveling or jet-lagged and least in the mood to make a phone call. Frequent travelers treat this as a single two-way booking rather than two separate errands, which also means dispatch already has your return flight number on file the moment your outbound trip starts.

## The Moment You Land

3. Turn your phone on before you're off the jet bridge if your airline allows it. A minute of extra notice helps your chauffeur time their approach to the pickup curb rather than waiting until you're already at the curb texting, which can add several unnecessary minutes to a trip that's otherwise perfectly timed.

4. Know your terminal and airline before you land, not after. It sounds minor, but it's the single most common thing that slows down a curbside meetup when a traveler isn't sure which door they're walking out of, especially at larger, multi-terminal airports like IAD where the walk between concourses can genuinely take several minutes on its own.

## Handling Delays Like a Pro

5. Don't call to report a minor delay if you booked with your flight number — the system already knows. Save the call for something the tracking wouldn't catch, like a gate change that affects which exit you'll use, or a change to which airport you're actually landing at due to a diversion. Trusting the system here saves you a call you don't need to make during an already stressful moment.

6. If a delay is going to be hours, not minutes, a quick heads-up call is still worth it so dispatch can plan the chauffeur's schedule around it rather than just waiting — this is especially true late at night, when overnight staffing benefits from advance notice. A three-hour weather delay handled with a courtesy call tends to go more smoothly than one where dispatch only finds out when the original pickup time has already passed.

## Groups and Luggage

7. Give an honest luggage count when you book, including bags that aren't obvious — golf clubs, car seats, oversized items, a second checked bag you added last-minute. It's the difference between the right vehicle showing up and a tight squeeze at the curb, and it takes the same amount of time to mention as it does to leave out.

8. For group trips, designate one point of contact for the chauffeur to reach, rather than everyone texting separately. It keeps communication simple if plans shift, and avoids the confusion of conflicting messages from different people in the same group — frequent group travelers usually default to whoever made the original booking staying the point of contact for the whole trip.

## Little Things That Matter

9. If you need a car seat, say so at booking, not at the curb — proper installation takes a few minutes and is done before the vehicle arrives, not improvised curbside while everyone waits and a line of other travelers watches. Mentioning the child's approximate age or weight at booking also helps make sure the right seat type shows up.

10. Save your car service's direct number in your phone before you travel, not after you need it. When something actually goes sideways — a cancelled flight, a gate change, a missed connection — a direct line beats hunting for a confirmation email buried in your inbox while your phone battery is at eight percent and you're standing in a rebooking line.

## Why These Small Habits Add Up

None of these ten tips are complicated on their own — that's part of why they're easy to overlook the first few times you fly. What makes them worth adopting as habits is that they each remove one small point of friction from a day that already has plenty of variables outside your control: weather, air traffic, a gate that changes twice. Handled well, ground transportation is the one part of the trip that should never be the thing that goes wrong.

Frequent travelers tend to arrive at these habits gradually, usually after one trip where skipping one of them caused a real headache. Adopting them upfront, rather than learning them the hard way, is really the whole point of a list like this one.

## A Bonus Habit: Building a Standing Travel Profile

Travelers who fly a few times a month tend to develop something like an informal profile with whichever car service they use regularly — preferred pickup spot at their home address, a usual vehicle class, maybe a standing note about a car seat or a mobility consideration. None of this is required for any individual booking, but it saves real time over dozens of trips a year, since fewer details need to be repeated each time.

If you find yourself booking the same kind of trip repeatedly — the same route to the same airport, the same vehicle class every time — mention that pattern to dispatch. It's a small thing, but frequent travelers consistently report that being a known, recurring customer rather than a first-time caller every trip makes each individual booking faster and smoother.

## Where These Habits Matter Most

Every one of these ten habits matters more during the specific moments when travel is already stressed — a weather delay, an early-morning departure bank, a holiday travel week, an unfamiliar airport on a connecting itinerary. On a calm Tuesday afternoon with no complications, almost any approach to booking ground transportation works fine. The value of good habits shows up precisely when something else about the trip is already going sideways, which is exactly when you want one part of the day to be handled without a second thought.

## Turning Tips Into Habits

The gap between reading a list like this once and actually benefiting from it is repetition — none of these ten habits require remembering all ten perfectly on your very next trip. Pick two or three that address whatever's caused you the most friction in the past (a missed connection, a confusing curbside pickup, a forgotten car seat request) and build those into your routine first. The rest tend to follow naturally once booking ahead and flight-number tracking become second nature.

Frequent flyers rarely think of any of this as a checklist by the time it's second nature — it just becomes how they travel. That's the actual goal: not memorizing ten rules, but reaching the point where ground transportation stops being something you have to think hard about at all.

## Putting These Tips Into Practice

The easiest way to start is on your very next trip: book with your flight number, confirm the return leg at the same time, and save the direct dispatch number in your phone before you leave the house. Those three alone cover the habits that prevent the most common day-of problems, and they take less time to do than reading this list did.

For any trip after that, request a quote online or call (877) 609-1919, mention anything unusual about the trip up front — group size, luggage, a car seat, a special occasion — and let dispatch handle the rest. The other habits on this list will start to feel automatic after a trip or two of doing them on purpose.

## The Common Thread

Look closely at all ten tips and a single theme runs through nearly every one: give the people handling your transportation more information, sooner, rather than less information, later. Flight numbers instead of times, honest luggage counts instead of rounded-down guesses, a heads-up call instead of silence during a long delay — none of it is complicated, and all of it makes the actual trip run more smoothly than showing up and hoping for the best. It's a small mindset shift that pays off on every single trip afterward, not just the occasional one where something goes wrong — and once it becomes habit, it barely takes any extra thought or time at all compared to booking without any of it in mind — which is really the point of building any good habit in the first place, travel-related or otherwise. The travelers who seem to glide through airports without incident aren't luckier than everyone else — they've simply internalized a short list of habits like these ten until following them stopped requiring any deliberate effort at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the single most useful habit on this list?**

Booking with your flight number instead of just a time — it's what lets automatic flight tracking do the work of adjusting your pickup, and it prevents most of the other issues on this list.

**Do I need to call if my flight is a few minutes late?**

No — flight-number bookings track this automatically. Save the call for changes tracking wouldn't catch, like a gate or exit change.

**What if I forgot to mention extra luggage when I booked?**

Call ahead as soon as you know — dispatch can often adjust the vehicle before pickup if there's enough notice.

**How do I add a car seat request after booking?**

Call (877) 609-1919 as soon as possible — car seats are inspected, sanitized, and installed before the vehicle arrives, which takes a few minutes of lead time.

**Is there really no charge for a normal deplaning delay?**

Correct — complimentary wait time (45 minutes domestic, 60 international) covers the normal time between landing and reaching the curb.
